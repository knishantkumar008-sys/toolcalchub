How to Calculate How Many Solar Panels You Need for Your Home (2026 Guide)

📅 March 2026⏱ 8 min read✍️ ToolCalcHub Team

One of the most common questions homeowners ask before going solar is: how many solar panels do I actually need? The answer depends on your electricity usage, your location, and the type of panels you choose. This guide walks you through the exact calculation — step by step.

Step 1: Find Your Monthly Electricity Usage

Look at your electricity bill and find your monthly kWh (kilowatt-hour) consumption. In the USA, the average home uses about 877 kWh/month (EIA 2025). In India, a typical household uses 200–400 kWh/month. In the UK, around 350–400 kWh/month is common.

Quick tip: If you don't have your bill handy, check your electricity meter over 30 days, or use our Solar Panel Size Calculator above — just enter an estimate.

Step 2: Find Your Peak Sun Hours (PSH)

Peak sun hours (PSH) represent the number of hours per day when sunlight is strong enough to produce maximum solar output (equivalent to 1,000 W/m²). This varies hugely by location:

Step 3: Apply the Real-World Derate Factor

Solar panels don't operate at 100% efficiency in the real world. Several factors reduce output:

  • Inverter efficiency: ~96% (modern string inverters)
  • Wiring losses: ~2% (DC cable resistance)
  • Soiling losses: ~3% (dust, bird droppings)
  • Temperature losses: ~5% (panels lose efficiency in high heat)

Combined, these give you a system derate factor of about 0.87 (87%). This is the standard used by NREL's PVWatts calculator.

Step 4: The Formula

Here's the complete formula our calculator uses:

Number of Panels = (Daily kWh ÷ PSH) ÷ (Panel Watts ÷ 1000 × Derate Factor)

Example: 500 kWh/month home in California (PSH 5.5), 400W panels, 80% stated efficiency:
Daily usage = 500 ÷ 30 = 16.67 kWh
Panel output = (400÷1000) × 5.5 × 0.87 = 1.91 kWh/day
Panels needed = 16.67 ÷ 1.91 = 9 panels

Panel Wattage Matters

Modern solar panels in 2026 range from 300W to 600W. The most common residential panels are 400W–450W monocrystalline, which offer the best balance of efficiency (20–22%) and cost. Higher wattage panels mean fewer panels needed but cost more per panel.

Panel Degradation Over Time

All solar panels lose a small amount of efficiency each year. Modern panels degrade at about 0.5% per year (NREL standard). After 25 years, your panels will produce about 88% of their original output — still very productive.
